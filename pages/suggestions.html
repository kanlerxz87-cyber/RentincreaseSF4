Informational compliance research, not legal advice. No attorney has approved a proposed increase and nothing here guarantees that a complaint, inquiry or lawsuit will not occur. Any increase is framed only as a conservative compliance checklist capable of tenant-lawyer review.

How to use this page

This page is a catalogue of proposals, not a set of changes. Nothing here has been implemented, and every row starts at the decision value Review. Read the rows, then fill in the Review checklist at the bottom of this page with Accept, Reject or Defer for each item. Only the items marked Accept are candidates for implementation.

How each row is sourced. A row that improves the site because an official rule requires something carries that rule's official source link. A row that is purely a proposal about how this site should work carries the marker Internal site-design proposal and no legal claim is made for it. No official source is cited merely to fill a cell.

What this page does not do. It does not state that any rent increase is lawful, approved or safe from challenge. It does not give legal advice. It does not decide anything about the two units in this case; it proposes changes to the research site so that the decision is better supported.

Priority convention used below. Effort is the build cost (Low / Medium / High). Impact is the expected effect on the quality of the decision (High / Medium / Low). Both are estimates about site work, not statements about law.

1. Compliance gates - make the required order of operations impossible to miss

Each proposal below adds a gate that must be answered before a notice is dated. Every one is grounded in a rule published by the San Francisco Rent Board, the Rent Ordinance, California statute or federal regulation.

ID / suggestionWhat changesEffortImpactOfficial basisDecision
SUG-01
License-status gate per unit		Add a per-unit row on the Anonymized Case page and on the Notice Checklist: 'Rent Increase License status for this unit, confirmed on (date), portal reference (optional)'. Leave it blank and flag it until the owner fills in a real date.

Why: The license is the one precondition that can void an otherwise lawful increase, and the rule tests the *effective date* of the increase, not the date the notice was served. A dated status field turns an invisible dependency into a visible gate.

LowHighRule 13.14: a rent increase purporting to be effective while the landlord is unlicensed renders the entire increase null and void, and the effective date - not the date of service - is the test. SF.gov: a landlord must have a current rent increase license on file before any annual or banked increase can go into effect.

Housing Inventory filing tracker		Add a per-unit filing row: 'Housing Inventory submitted for reporting year (YYYY) on (date)'. Show the recurring March 1 deadline and the rule that units in buildings with fewer than 10 residential units have reported since March 1, 2023.

Why: The rent increase license is generated from the Housing Inventory submission, so the filing is the upstream cause of every license gap. Tracking it removes the most common way a license goes missing.

LowHighSF.gov: all owners of residential property are required to report into the Housing Inventory by March 1 every year; reporting began March 1, 2023 for condominiums and units in buildings with fewer than 10 residential units; the license is generated from the report.

Rent Board Fee calendar with the tenant-recovery switch		Add a fee timeline to the Rent Board Fee page (October informational notice, December exemption window, January invoice, March 1 due date, 5% monthly late penalties capped at 15%, June 1 referral) plus one yes/no field per unit: 'Was the tenant in occupancy on November 1 of the applicable tax year?' That answer alone should decide whether the 50% recovery may be charged.

Why: The fee is a separate money decision from the rent increase, it has its own penalty ladder, and the right to recover half of it turns on a single November 1 occupancy test. Treating it as a calendar plus a switch removes a recurring source of error.

LowMediumSF.gov Rent Board Fee page: $59.00 per dwelling unit for 2026-2027, due March 1, 5% late penalty each month capped at 15%, referral to the Bureau of Delinquent Revenue after June 1, exemption request deadline December 14, 2026. 50% may be recovered only from tenants in occupancy on November 1 of the applicable tax year.

Why: The rate in effect on the effective date controls, not the rate in effect when the notice is written, and the currently published 1.6% rate covers only March 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027. A date-driven selector prevents picking the wrong period.

MediumHighSF.gov: the percentage is the one in effect on the date the increase takes effect; 1.6% applies to increases effective March 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027, and Section 37.3 caps the increase at 7% of CPI.

Banked-increase ledger per unit		Add a per-unit table that logs every increase the owner could have taken and did not take: the year, the anniversary date, the published percentage at that time, and whether 24 full months elapsed without any increase. Show the running banked total and the notice wording that must state the banked portion.

Why: Banking requires the 24-full-month test for each skipped year, forbids compounding and prorating, and never carries to a new tenant. It is the hardest part of the calculation to hold in memory and the easiest to misstate in a notice.

MediumHighSF.gov: a skipped increase is banked only if at least 24 full months pass without any increase; banked percentages are added, never compounded or prorated, and the notice must state the banked portion.

SUG-07
Retaliation-window screen		Add a short screening block that must be answered before any notice is dated: 'In the last 12 months, has any tenant complained to an agency or exercised a right under the tenant chapter?', with a note of the answer and the date checked.

Why: A rent increase that follows a protected complaint or act can be challenged as retaliation within the statutory window, independently of whether the increase amount itself is permitted. The bar is time-based and easy to miss on a case that has had a repair or complaint history.

LowHighCalifornia Civil Code 1942.5(a): a landlord may not retaliate against a lessee who has exercised rights under the chapter or complained to an agency within 12 months of the complaint; Section 37.10B separately prohibits bad-faith harassment.

Uniform-criteria record for the one-unit-or-both decision		Add a field that records the single neutral eligibility criterion applied to each unit (for example: the unit's anniversary date has passed), the date each anniversary passed, and the owner-entered reason if only one unit is raised.

Why: No official rule requires raising both units. What a reviewer would look for is a consistent, documented, non-pretextual reason applied the same way to both units, which is what the fair-housing rules ask when a neutral practice has a disparate effect.

LowHigh24 CFR 100.500: a practice with a disparate effect violates the Fair Housing Act unless it is supported by a substantial legitimate nondiscriminatory interest and no less discriminatory alternative is available. California Government Code 12955 and the California Civil Rights Department housing guidance add source-of-income and retaliation protections.

Why: If a unit is not covered by rent control the rent increase limitations do not apply to it and every other gate changes. This is the first gate and the one currently resting on an assumption rather than a record.

LowHighSF.gov: many units built on or before June 13, 1979 that are not single-family dwellings have rent control, and partial exemptions exist for newly constructed units and for certain single-family homes and condominiums; the Property Information Map is the official lookup.

MediumHighCalifornia Civil Code 827: at least 30 days' notice where the increase is 10% or less and 90 days where it exceeds 10%, delivered personally or by mail, and the notice must state the amount of the increase and its effective date.

Why: Each petition category has its own pre-notice filing rule, and the filing for one type is not a substitute for another. A routing table stops the wrong petition from being filed, or none being filed when one is required.

Case-file completeness checklist		Add a checklist of the documents an owner should be able to produce for each unit: the current lease, every prior increase notice, proof of service for each notice, the license status record, the Housing Inventory submission receipt, the Rent Board Fee payment receipt, the Property Information Map printout, and the rate sheet actually used for the calculation.

3. Discovering the missing information

The owner's standard is that a decision should rest on what is already known and what is not yet known. These proposals turn the open blanks into an ordered discovery plan, and separate the facts that only the owner can supply from the ones that a public record can answer.

Missing-information register		Add one page that lists every open question in this case with five columns: what is missing, which official source would resolve it, how to obtain it, who must supply it, and which decision it blocks. Populate it from the existing needs-review rows so nothing stays only on the Flagged for Review page.

Why: Every unresolved item currently appears as a blank cell on several pages. A single register turns a scatter of blanks into an ordered work plan, and it is the direct answer to what information has to exist before a decision can reasonably be made.

MediumHighThe official routes that would resolve the open items are the portal and Housing Inventory pages, the Property Information Map, the Section 6.14 guidance, the counsel line and the Tenant Petition process.
